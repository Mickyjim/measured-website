A guide for difficult messages

How to set a boundary over text

State what you can or cannot do, keep the wording specific, and offer an alternative only if you want to. A boundary works best when it describes an action you can follow through on.

Make the limit clear and realistic

You can be warm without making the limit uncertain. Avoid long explanations if they distract from your decision. Where you have shared responsibilities, account for the practical arrangements that still need attention.

A short example

Illustrative wording to adapt to your situation

Message received
“Call me now. I don't care how late it is.”
A possible reply
“I'm not available for a call tonight. I can speak tomorrow after work.”

It gives a clear limit and a possible next step. Use a different time, or omit the alternative, if that is more accurate for you.

Five boundary texts you can adapt

A useful boundary names your decision and what you will do next. It does not need to persuade the other person to agree. Choose wording you can honestly follow through on.

When someone asks for a favour you cannot take on

“I can't take that on this week. I hope you find someone who can help.”

Leave out an alternative if you do not have one. “Maybe later” can create an expectation you do not mean to set.

When a friend keeps calling late at night

“I'm putting my phone away for the evening. I'll read your message tomorrow.”

State your availability rather than arguing about whether their request is reasonable. If this is a repeated issue, give your usual calling hours when you next talk.

When the conversation turns insulting

“I'm willing to talk about what happened. I'm going to pause this conversation while we're calling each other names.”

Then pause. Sending a long final argument undermines the limit you just set. For a specific workplace request, see replying to a rude coworker.

When someone pushes after you have said no

“I understand you're disappointed. My answer is still no.”

Acknowledge the reaction without reopening your decision. You can repeat the limit once, then stop debating it. You do not need a new explanation for every objection.

When an ex wants a personal conversation

“I'm not available to talk about our relationship. If there's a practical arrangement we need to sort out, please send the details.”

Use the practical-arrangements line only if it applies. Shared parenting, belongings or other responsibilities may need a clear channel. See deciding how to reply to an ex for other situations.

What if the reply sounds too harsh?

Read it once for clarity and once for tone. Remove insults, guesses about motives and threats you do not intend to carry out. You can add “Thanks for asking” or “I understand” when sincere, but keep the decision visible. A clear no can disappoint someone without being rude.

Do I have to respond again?

If you have answered the actual question and nothing practical has changed, another explanation may not help. A pause is an option. If you do need to coordinate something, answer that part directly and keep the boundary separate.
